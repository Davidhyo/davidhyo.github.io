About

I like understanding the people I design for.

I’m originally from the Republic of Benin in West Africa, but I’ve spent most of my life in Washington, D.C. Now in Atlanta, I get to explore what I love most: stories, the arts, and the many cultures that shape how people live. I grew up speaking Ewe and French. I learned English and picked up Spanish along the way. Growing up between languages taught me that one word can mean very different things, so I try to meet people in the language they’re most at home in.

My process starts with listening. I interview and survey the people I’m designing for and study the world around them, so I work on their problem and not the one I assume they have. I look for perspectives different from mine, including my teammates, so that my research informs meaningful design.

Case study · Social impact, service design

Project Wash Bus

A mobile laundry service that gave Atlanta's unhoused population regular access to clean clothes.

The Wash Bus parked outside Grady Hospital in downtown Atlanta. Photo: Georgia Tech College of Design.

Partner
Flowing with Blessings Inc., Georgia Tech School of Industrial Design
My role
Industrial Designer: machine securing and maintenance access
Timeline
Jul – Dec 2023
Team
Five industrial design students, Georgia Tech summer studio
The problem
The unhoused in Atlanta lack regular access to clean clothes, a basic need that makes it harder for them to find work and keep it.
What we did
We turned a retired school bus into a mobile laundromat with seven washer-dryer pairs, a volunteer station, and water storage.
The result
More than 4,500 people served by September 2024, two months after the bus hit the Atlanta streets.

MethodsOwner interviewsStakeholder mapFMVSS and EPA reviewLoad calculationsLocal rent and homelessness data

The problem

As housing costs in Atlanta rise, so does the unhoused population, and a lack of clean clothes is an overlooked barrier to interviewing and keeping a job.

Flowing with Blessings, a nonprofit that already ran mobile showers, saw people put the same soiled clothes back on afterwards. Its founder bought a school bus and asked Georgia Tech to design a mobile laundry.

Research and insights

We mapped the people the bus had to serve at once, and paired that with research on local rent and homelessness data that helped us see how interconnected this subject was to their day-to-day lives.

We understoodThe bus has to work for guests, volunteers and location owners at once.

So we designedA volunteer workstation and locked supply storage alongside the machines.

We understoodHeavy machines shift with the vibration and turns of daily driving, and vehicle safety rules apply.

So we designedA securing system of load tracks and window jamb supports.

We understoodOne broken machine can stop the service for the day.

So we designedPull-out mounts, so volunteers can reach a machine for repair.

My part: securing the machines

What I owned

I designed how the washers and dryers stay fixed through the vibration and turns of daily driving, and how volunteers can still reach them for maintenance. I researched FMVSS and EPA regulations to set the mounting requirements.

Maintenance access optionRated load
Option 1: linear rails with a traction mat350 lb
Option 2: heavy-duty drawer slides270 lb

The design

The finished bus holds seven washer-dryer pairs plus two larger units, with a volunteer workstation, supply storage and water tanks fitted into a repurposed bus.

Outcome

4,500+

people served by September 2024. The bus launched in July 2024.

The nonprofit used our design decks, calculations and implementation proposal to raise funds and build the bus. It now parks outside Grady Hospital every week, a second bus is in development, and it was shown at Georgia Tech's design showcase in May 2025.

Coverage: Georgia Tech News ↗ · Georgia Tech School of Industrial Design ↗ · Core77 ↗ · Atlanta Daily World ↗

Reflection

I found my passion for design that helps people improve their lives, design that helps people become a better version of themselves.

Case study · Healthcare, research-driven design

MediHive

A grief tool that helps ICU nurses process loss and rebuild unity within their unit.

Partner
Institute for Patient-Centered Design
My role
Industrial Designer: interviews, ideation, CAD, lighting
Timeline
Fall 2021
Team
Four industrial designers, Georgia Tech Junior Health Design Studio
The problem
ICU nurses absorb patient loss constantly, with almost no time or space to grieve it.
What we did
Interviewed six nurses and caregivers, compared existing grief products, and developed four concepts into one modular wall for the break room.
The result
A working prototype, with manufacturing, assembly and maintenance worked out for the Institute.

MethodsInterviews with six nurses and caregiversShift journey mapStakeholder mapPrivacy-rule reviewThree personasFour concepts compared

The problem

ICU nurses face emotionally draining work every shift, and COVID-19 made it heavier. A 2017 CareerBuilder survey reported about 70% of nurses felt burnout in their current role, and few units have anything for the grief that builds up. The Institute for Patient-Centered Design asked our team to design a grief tool for them.

Research

We interviewed six nurses and caregivers with 7 to 27 years in the job, mapped a full shift and the stakeholders in a hospital, and reviewed the privacy rules that limit what nurses can share.

The nurses coped in very different ways, so we built three personas.

15+ years of experience

April

Three young kids at home. Her unit knows her as "the strong one." She comforts others while keeping her own emotions under control.

New to the field

Ana

Graduated during COVID. She has had less time to build ways of coping.

20+ years of experience

Susan

Struggling with burnout. She hides it at work and cries in the bathroom.

Personas are fictional. Photos are for illustration only.

Existing grief products, from journals to support groups, were mostly general wellness tools, and few were made for nurses.

Nurses told us they grieve alone or with one trusted coworker, so we designed a shared wall where they leave notes and small gifts for each other. They barely get thirty minutes to themselves in a shift, so it had to take seconds to use. And because knowing they aren't forgotten lifts morale, the wall has a magnet board for thank-you cards from patients' families.

Choosing a direction

We sketched widely, sorted the ideas by type and marked the strongest.

We scored four concepts against six goals. None met all six, so the final design combines the strongest parts of each into a modular system of hexagons.

The design

MediHive is a modular wall of hexagons in three sizes. Each hexagon does one job, so a unit can grow and change with the team.

Dry-erase boardCubbyMagnetic wallLightDoorCentral hub

Before building in acrylic, we tested the idea in cardboard: cubby depth, how the door opens, and whether leaving a note felt natural.

Outcome

MediHive is a working prototype built from what six nurses and caregivers told us. Manufacturing, assembly and maintenance are worked out for the Institute.

The prototype has not been tested in a hospital yet. The next step is a real break room and feedback from nurses.

Case study · Automotive, accessibility

Project NODE

A captain's chair and work surface for a single-seat autonomous vehicle, built for wheelchair riders from the start.

Partner
General Motors, Georgia Tech HMI Lab
My role
Need-finding, anthropometrics, concepts and user testing
Timeline
Aug – Dec 2022
Team
Mechanical engineers and industrial designers
The problem
Autonomous vehicle concepts rarely work for wheelchair riders without a retrofit.
What we did
Researched riders' needs and body dimensions, then designed a chair and work surface that take a standard seat or a wheelchair in the same shell.
The result
A full-scale plywood prototype of the chair and work platform for the GM platform, with specifications, materials and load calculations.

MethodsRider survey and interviews, 23 peopleAnthropometric analysisConversation with Q’StraintMockup tests with 5 peoplePersonas and journey mapConcept evaluation matrix, 7 conceptsHouse of quality

The problem

Most autonomous vehicle designs fall short on space efficiency and on real accessibility for riders with disabilities. With General Motors, we asked what a single-seat vehicle, a NODE, could look like if accessibility came first.

Research

I led the need-finding. I wrote a survey and interviewed 23 people about how they ride, what they do from a seat, and what they want from it. I also ran the anthropometric analysis and built personas and journey maps. Research analysis led us to three use cases: studying, sleeping, and eating on the move.

We built three fictional personas to keep the riders in view: Jason, a 30-year-old product lead who spends 14 hours a week traveling; Elena, a 21-year-old international student balancing school, work and family; and Michael, a 48-year-old software developer whose daughter needs help getting around.

We also met with Q’Straint, a supplier of wheelchair securement systems, for an in-depth conversation and asked key questions that settled a big decision: which lock would secure a wheelchair. We chose their QLK-150 locking system, which is rated to 20g in crash tests.

The vehicle could not be two products bolted together: the same shell had to take a standard seat or a wheelchair, so every dimension had to work from the 5th percentile female to the 95th percentile male.

We foundRiders want to study, sleep and eat while the vehicle drives.

So we designedA fold-away work surface with room for a laptop and papers.

We foundThe heaviest load on the surface is a rider leaning on it to sleep.

So we designedA platform and hinges rated for 60 lb, with a safety factor of 2.5.

We foundWheelchair riders and standard-seat riders need the same vehicle.

So we designedA 36 in console opening that takes either a wheelchair or the swivel seat.

We foundA wheelchair rider has to be secured in a moving vehicle by a proven locking system.

So we designedA seat that comes out and gives way to a wheelchair, locked in with the Q’Straint QLK-150.

Some requirements from the researchValue
Minimum seat width28.25 in
Minimum seat depth18.5 in
Minimum backrest height39.9 in
Seat angle100–110°
Console opening, so any wheelchair fits36 in

Testing

With a cardboard console, a cardboard work platform and a knob prototype, I tested the controls and the platform with five people. They ranged from late teens to over 60, from 5'0" to 5'11", and included left- and right-handed riders. Among the many questions I asked were where their hands would want the controls and how they would grab the platform. I also watched what they did first.

What five testers did and saidResult
Wanted the controls under the platform2 of 5
Wanted the controls where the hand naturally falls3 of 5
Compared the platform's movement to a computer mouse3 of 5
Asked for a slight tilt to the platform3 of 5
Asked for a drink or pencil holder3 of 5
Asked for adjustability, sliding or modularity3 of 5

Small sample, five people. We used it to catch problems early, not to prove a design.

The vehicle had to adapt to the rider, not the other way around.

The design

The final concept centers on a captain's chair and a fold-away walnut work surface, built as an interchangeable module for the GM autonomous platform.

The chair sits on a flat swivel base, so riders can turn to enter and exit. The base is rated for 450 lb, well above the 340 lb we designed for: a 290 lb 95th percentile male plus the 50 lb seat. It sits under the center of the seat to save space. A locking pin holds the chair still while the vehicle moves, and slides let it shift from side to side. When a rider needs the space for a wheelchair, the chair comes out and the wheelchair locks in its place.

Building it

We built a functional prototype full scale, in plywood on a swivel seat base, to check how the fold-away work platform, the side console and the seat turn would feel in use. The final concept calls for walnut, but the budget did not allow it, and a functional prototype was all we needed to test the ideas. A fabrication package describes how the final design would be assembled: the platform slide, the console, a chair swivel with a locking pin, and a Type 1 seatbelt, with a wheelchair locking mechanism as the alternative to the chair. The prototype was shown at a project showcase.

Outcome

A standard seat and a wheelchair can share one seat module and one work surface. We built it full size in plywood to test how it fits and folds.

Reflection

Working with mechanical engineers taught me that the best teams are the ones that do not avoid the storming phase. Designing for people's needs starts with understanding the needs of the people closest to me, my own teammates.

Case study · Service design, data storytelling

The New Holland Experience

A concept for a brewery that guests want to return to, and that learns from every visit.

Subject
New Holland Brewing (studio concept)
My role
Industrial Designer: furniture CAD, market distribution research and data visualization
Timeline
Spring 2022
Team
Three industrial designers and one architect, Georgia Tech joint studio
The problem
New Holland's taprooms looked like any other brewery, and the company learned little about the people drinking there.
What we did
Designed the whole visit: the building, the guest's journey, a mobile app, a bartender's view and a plan for the data.
The result
A complete concept presented at final review, with a business case for where the brewery could grow next.

MethodsBrand and competitor researchThree personasDistribution mappingFurniture CAD

The problem

New Holland's beer was sold across the country, but its taprooms looked like every other brewery and the company learned little about its drinkers. Our question: how might we create a brewery experience that stands out and collects useful data along the way?

Research

Four themes from early research on the brand and its competitors shaped the proposal.

Decentralized brandingPhysical plus digitalPersonalized experienceOur community

We built the service around three people.

Guest · 24 · Designer

Amy

Loves trying new places, but every brewery in town feels the same. She forgets what she ordered and whether she liked it.

Guest · 33 · Accountant

Jeff

Wants a traditional night out and a beer he likes. He is picky and struggles with technology.

Staff · 39 · Bartender

Michael

Loves talking with regulars. In a rush he has no time for them, and he finds it hard to recommend beers he doesn't know.

Guests forget what they ordered and what they liked, so the app learns each guest's tastes and recommends a drink. Not every guest wants to use technology, so the bar also works without the app, with the bartender carrying the recommendations. And because bartenders lose their time with guests during a rush, a seat-by-seat screen suggests drinks and flags who needs help.

The place

The building is organized into zones, from an open bar for all generations to bookable immersive rooms whose walls change with the game or mood. The aim was the Dutch idea of gezellig: warm and shared.

The guest's side

A guest signs up once and picks the flavors they like. The app then recommends drinks, remembers what they tried and lets them book a room.

The staff's side

The same information reaches the bartender: a map of the bar shows what each seat is drinking, what to suggest next and who needs help.

The data

Each touchpoint collects something different. The team mapped what overlaps and how preferences turn into suggestions.

The business case

Knowing who drinks what, and where they come from, tells New Holland where to open next and where to send which products.

Outcome

We presented a complete concept at final review: the building, the guest journey, the app, the staff tools and a business case. It was not built or tested with guests; the next step would be to prototype the app and the bartender view with real customers and staff.

Case study · Soft goods, social impact

Incognito

A backpack for people living unsheltered, designed to keep valuables out of sight and safe from theft.

Context
Georgia Tech soft goods studio
My role
Solo: research, concepts, prototype and final model
Timeline
Fall 2020
Team
Solo project
The problem
Unsheltered people have few safe ways to carry what they own, and theft is a real risk.
What we did
Researched a day in the life of two users, set three design criteria, compared five concepts, and tested a paper prototype on the body.
The result
A final model and materials direction for a pack that hides its contents. A concept, not built in fabric.

MethodsTwo user profiles, a day in the lifeThree design criteriaFive concepts comparedPaper prototype worn on the body

The problem

Left: from The Washington Post opinion piece "Family Homelessness in D.C. Is Growing." Right: from a 2011 SlideShare presentation, "Prayer for Homeless Persons." Neither source credits a photographer.

Crimes against homeless people are rising. In Los Angeles, an LAPD report found robberies against homeless people up 89% and larceny up 86% from 2017 to 2018. The brief: design a portable storage bag that protects their valuables from theft and robbery while staying easy to carry.

I built two user profiles, a 20-year-old who has been homeless for two years and a 24-year-old who recently became homeless, and listed what each guards most, from a photo of a mother to medicine and ID documents.

Research

The pack must not draw attention, so I hid the access to the valuables. The day is spent on the move, in and out of storage, so the opening takes two steps or fewer. It has to be tolerable to wear all day, so I chose a flexible material with latches that hold it to the body.

The design

I sketched five concepts and compared them against three criteria: function, ease of use and security. Concept A met all three.

A paper prototype worn on the body let me test the shape before I modeled the final.

Outcome

A final model and materials direction for a pack that hides its contents. It is a concept: it was not built in fabric or tested with the people it is for.

Reflection

Next time I would get feedback from people in person earlier, plan materials ahead of time, and learn the modeling software before starting.
